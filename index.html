May I humbly present to you a brief video introduction about myself.

PRACTICE

Movement II

Practice

The second movement develops the beat and frequently highlights the drumline. Visually it involves intricate drill or body movements, keeping the energy high but shifting the musical style away from the intro.

Before sunrise, in the autumn fog, a marching band is already on the field practicing. The metronome clicks at 140 BPM. Long before the first bell, you show up ready, because the future performance demands it.

That same discipline and dedication, line by line - point by point, is how I have approached engineering from day one.

The Mobile Switching Office I built started from a gravel lot. Trunk lines ordered, foundation poured, a secure room built within a building to house the racks, power, and redundancy that a live network requires. Switches, Voice Mail Systems, Base Station Controllers, HVAC… every component installed, integrated, and rigorously tested before a single call could be made.

Like a band that earns its halftime show’s impeccability through thousands of repetitions, that foundation earned me Nextel’s Circle of Excellence award and a promotion to a National Technical Support Engineer.

From there, the stakes only grew. A telecom outage costs millions per hour and disrupts services that people’s lives depend on. On September 11th, 2001, Nextel’s dispatch network was one of the few communication channels still working for first responders. We provided real-time status updates to police, FBI, NSA, and DHS.

I supported that national network 24/7, managing outages from triage to root cause, coordinating vendors and cross-functional teams, and building internal tooling that improved how the entire team operated.

In marching band, the seniors make it look effortless. What you don’t see are the years of 6am practices that got them there, and the skillset they have honed.

Someone who stays composed when the systems go down and every second counts. Someone who can diagnose a problem they’ve never seen before, with incomplete information, while stakeholders are waiting on the line. Someone who can lead a bridge call with vendors, engineers, and federal agencies without losing the thread. And someone who never stops learning, because technology never stops changing.

Like marching band, through practice, I’ve learned to perform when it matters most.

On Monday, November 9th, 2026, Applications AE Brian Roche sent a Slack message at 4:17pm: “Hey man, have an emergency, can you join a Toyota outage call?” Toyota had been down since 8am, and on the outage bridge since 10am.

Toyota dealers rely on Tableau dashboards embedded in Salesforce Experience Cloud to run their business — accessed thousands of times per hour across their entire dealer network. Six hours in, the team still had no resolution.

By 5:17pm, I had identified and resolved the Tableau Server misconfiguration that had brought it all down. Dealer operations restored.

Sr. Manager of Technical Support Emily Cowan reached out afterward: “Thank you for jumping on today, really really appreciate it.”

PERFORMANCE

Movement III

Performance

The Ballad. This is where the music breathes. The tempo slows, the melody opens up, and what was technical becomes felt. The color guard takes the floor, sweeping flags, sabers catching light and the audience leans in.

My Tableau journey started the same way. As a performance engineer at Sprint, I used Tableau to find what the network data was hiding; time trends, signal strength along interstates, storm outage predictions mapped against NOAA weather. For the first time, we could see and feel the network. Tableau didn’t just show data. It told stories.

In marching band, sometimes the instrument chooses the player. When the performance engineering team needed a Tableau Server Administrator, my background in networking, server infrastructure, and systems made me the natural fit. I stepped into the role.

Tableau Server was on release 8. I still have that admin guide. I architected expansions, built performance monitoring, managed virtualization, and grew into the platform. At my first Tableau Conference in Washington D.C., I was a VIP customer. We had one of the largest Tableau deployments at the time. I got a special wristband allowing for drinks on a rooftop with Christian Chabot, Pat Hanrahan, and Chris Stolte. I said a total of one word to Christian: “Hi.” and I only smiled at Pat.

I was just a freshman.

By the next conference, I had grown into something different. On the plane, I pulled out a Tableau book. The woman next to me asked what I did with Tableau. When I told her I was a Tableau Server Administrator at Sprint, she smiled and said, “Oh, we need you.”

She was from BJC Healthcare, the largest healthcare provider in Missouri. Their Supply Chain Tableau Server, supporting 17 hospitals, was crashing daily. They had four full-time Tableau Professional Services consultants building dashboards, but no one with server expertise.

After my interview, one of those consultants told her Director: “He knows more about Tableau Server than anyone in this building. Including us.”

I knew how to play. I leapt at the chance to perform as a soloist.

What I hadn’t learned yet was that technical skill alone doesn’t move organizations.

BJC Supply Chain was operating as shadow IT with a small Tableau deployment sitting under someone’s desk, mostly invisible to the VP of IT whose enterprise BI platform of choice was IBM Cognos. My Director believed in Tableau. The VP did not. I had the technical answer. I didn’t yet have the influence to deliver it.

So I changed my approach. I enrolled in BJC’s MBA cohort. I ran Tableau training days. I launched a monthly Tableau User Group. I connected Washington University’s medical research team to our server. Tableau adoption grew until hospital executives, not just analysts, were depending on it for critical decisions.

The VP of IT invited me to lunch. Then to his project meetings. Then offered me a role in his org.

I declined the role. I accepted the platform.

He opened a formal Tableau vs. Cognos evaluation. Tableau won. He then launched an enterprise-wide Tableau project with me leading it alongside his IT managers. We architected a fully redundant, highly available Tableau Server infrastructure. We implemented it. I trained his team and handed over the keys.

That was the performance. Everything before it was rehearsal.

My Director left for Charter Communications and recruited me to do it again. Then Justin Swanson, one of those original BJC Tableau consultants, now a friend, told me about an opening at Tableau itself.

Tableau hired me as a Solution Architect.

The soloist had joined the ensemble.

I was now a Tableau Data Rockstar. They gave me a shirt to prove it. Two weeks at Tableau Bootcamp in Seattle, surrounded by new hires from every corner of the company; Sales, Engineering, Marketing, Support. Like learning what holds the props together backstage, we saw where the screws were a little loose. What bonded us was the shared belief that we were here to help people see and understand data.

Consulting was a different instrument than I was used to playing.

My background made me comfortable with pressure and unpredictability. What I hadn’t experienced was how compressed the performance window was. Four and a half days on-site to complete Business planning, IT architecture, installation, integration, security, training, and handoff. There weren’t weeks to build rapport, I had minutes to hours.

The only way it works is to build trust immediately by being authentic.

At L.S. Starrett, a globally respected precision tool maker in Athol, Massachusetts, I walked into skepticism. My SOW was a straightforward Tableau Server install. But when I asked the Manager of Information Systems what success actually looked like to him, I discovered the real problem: executives had mandated Tableau after their Brazil manufacturing team adopted it, but no one at HQ had a plan or buy-in.

I asked if their Brazil team could join us the next morning. By a stroke of luck, the Brazil team was at HQ for meetings.

In that meeting, I aligned both teams around analytics tied directly to their strategic initiatives, landing quick wins and business value. By Friday, Tableau Server was live, dashboards were built, governance was in place, and executives were receiving daily subscriptions of actionable KPIs.

Before I left, the Manager pulled me aside: “Please, keep doing what you do, and the way you do it.”

His follow-up email to my manager:

“This engagement was one of the best deliveries I have been a part of — thanks to your professionalism, knowledge, and people skills. You have definitely given us a jump start into a complex environment.”
— Mark Sprague, Manager of Information Systems, L.S. Starrett

Later in my tenure, I was being sent in to rescue red engagements.

Walmart had implemented Tableau Server for critical business decisions and was approaching their holiday network brownout, the worst possible time for Tableau performance issues to strike. A skilled Tableau consultant and technical account manager were already deep in the logs, analyzing Tableau processes, adjusting configurations, but with no progress being made.

They were brilliant people. But they had never been a customer operating an enterprise Tableau system.

Walmart didn’t want an analysis of the product. They wanted Tableau to work.

I quickly identified the problem. Tableau Server had been installed on shared virtualized hardware. Tableau is like a three year old, it hates sharing. I worked with their admins to migrate competing services off the shared hardware, then partnered with their infrastructure team to provision a dedicated cluster and re-architect the instance. Performance stabilized. They made it through the holidays, and had a path forward.

When Michelle Schleyer later asked me to give a Tableau MCP overview to Walmart, she slacked me afterward:

“Amazing job — clearly they love you. They can be a VERY tough crowd.”

The trust was already there.

Every year, the marching band retires the show and builds a new one. New music, new drill, new props, new challenge.

The defining engagement of my Professional Services career was Globe Life.

A three month engagement to install Tableau Server in a highly available distributed instance, integrate with AWS, and embed dashboards into Salesforce. The dashboards would deliver near real-time sales performance updates to thousands of independent field sales agents whose commissions were calculated against tightly controlled KPIs. Tableau wasn’t the product they were delivering. Tableau was the engine inside it. Users were never supposed to know it was there.

I became the Band Director.

The architecture I drew on their whiteboard, which you glimpsed in my intro video, touched nearly every layer of their technology stack: Active Directory with two-way subdomain trusts, mainframe exports, SQL Server Enterprise Data Warehouse replicated in AWS with Kerberos, Salesforce Visualforce development, Informatica ETL, Tableau REST and Embedding API programming, twice daily user provisioning, row-level security, hierarchical content permissions, and a first-ever implemented highly available dual-server Tableau Sparkler configuration for trusted SSO.

Globe Life didn’t just buy software. They bought a fully choreographed performance. Every system in formation, every handoff timed, every integration tested under load.

The engagement was so successful that Globe Life extended my contract for 18 months. Finally Tableau informed them that it could no longer be renewed. I wrote an 80 page handoff document and handed over the keys.

When my Director left Tableau for Informatica and offered to bring me with him, I chose to stay.

Instead of leaving Tableau, I moved to presales and became a Lead Solution Engineer on the Tableau Embedded Analytics team.

The Band Director took the podium.
